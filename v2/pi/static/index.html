Where the shelf comes from

Personal access token: discogs.com › Settings › Developers. The collection gives you the sleeves; the tracklists are a second pass, one request per record, and they are what lets a pressing you own overrule a service naming some greatest-hits. Everything that knows a record has sides comes from them — being asked which of three pressings is on, the A4 · Angels on the panel, the next track named before this one ends. The glass

A fine angle corrects a panel that ended up a few degrees off in its mount. It turns the finished picture, text and all, which costs about 17% more per redraw — measured, not guessed. Zero is the fast path and changes nothing; going from zero to any other value needs a restart, and there is a button for that below.

The main zone going dark is the signal — so switching off with the remote works, and so does the Apple TV turning the receiver off over HDMI. Touching the glass or turning the knob wakes it again, and so does switching the amplifier back on.

Brightness follows your finger, because setting a brightness you cannot see is a trick nobody has managed. The fine angle is in tenths of a degree, for a panel that came out of its mount not quite level and a bracket that is not coming off again. Still press save, or it reverts on the next restart.

Gain

Half a decibel a click is for the last hair of gain; two or three gets you there before the intro is over. The ceiling is a hand on your shoulder — 0 dB is wide open, and a receiver at 0 dB with a stylus in the run-out groove is a story you only tell once. The amplifier counts in half decibels and will take any of them, so which ones it lands on is purely about what you can stand to look at: some people cannot leave a −40.5 alone, and we do not judge. A click always moves, and in the direction you turned.

The fan steps up at 50, 60, 67.5 and 75 °C; throttling only starts at 80. Warmth is not a fault here, it is a small computer doing arithmetic on music. The panel shows a warning symbol from 75 °C.

Where it listens

A Denon or Marantz receiver digitises its analog inputs and serves each one over HTTP — the machinery behind sharing an input with HEOS speakers. That is what this listens on instead of a microphone, and it is why there is no microphone. Which input your turntable is on differs per rack, and the names the stream uses are not the ones on the front panel: PHONO is phono, but MPLAY is mediaplayer and SAT/CBL is cable_sat. So the list below comes from your receiver rather than from a table in here.

This is the part that matters. Every play hands over another stretch of the side, so the more a record gets used the less anybody outside the house is asked about it — and a record it knows itself is named in under a second, offline, for ever. It stops at about three and a half minutes per record, which is not tidiness: without a ceiling one favourite grows into a third of the database and starts answering for everything else.

A second opinion

Optional, paid, and most people will not have it. Shazam and the fingerprints downstairs carry this between them; AudD is a second pair of ears on the same recording, for whoever would rather not lean on an unofficial client and has the budget for the principle. A key that has run out is left alone for a day rather than asked about every record.

For the record

Vinyl is the hole in every listening history: streaming counts itself, a record leaves nothing behind but the memory of getting up. With a ListenBrainz token every recognised record is logged with its artist, track, album and the side it was on. Nothing is sent while this is empty.
